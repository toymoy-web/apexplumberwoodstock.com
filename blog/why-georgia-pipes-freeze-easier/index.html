Why A Brief Cold Snap Does More Damage In Georgia Than A Whole Winter Up North

By the Woodstock Apex Plumbing team · January 15, 2026

It seems like it should be the other way around. A place with real, sustained winters should have more frozen-pipe problems than a place where freezing weather lasts a few days a year. In practice, the opposite is often true, and the reason comes down to how homes are actually built, not how cold it actually gets on the coldest day of the year.

In a state where winter is the norm, building codes, insulation standards, and pipe placement all assume freezing temperatures are coming every year. Pipes get buried below the frost line, insulated as standard practice, and routed away from exterior walls wherever possible.

Georgia's building practices were never built around that assumption, because for most winters, they did not need to be. Pipes in crawlspaces, garages, and exterior walls here are frequently uninsulated, not because of a shortcut, but because insulating for a freeze that might not come in a given year has historically been a lower priority than it would be somewhere colder.

There's also a behavioral factor worth naming honestly: homeowners in a place where hard freezes are rare simply don't develop the habits, the automatic faucet-dripping, the reflexive cabinet-door-opening, that become second nature somewhere winter arrives every year without fail. That's not a criticism, it's just a different set of habits shaped by a different climate, and it means those habits have to be learned deliberately here rather than absorbed automatically.

The other factor is duration and warning. A sustained northern winter gives homeowners weeks to prepare and a heating system that is already running hard. A Georgia cold snap can drop temperatures 20 or 30 degrees in a single day, catching a home's heating system, and its owner, off guard.

None of this means Georgia homes are worse-built. It means the calculus of how much to prepare for a freeze is genuinely different here, and a lot of homeowners never think about it until the first hard freeze of the season catches them without a plan.

Homebuilders in genuinely cold-winter states also design plumbing layouts to avoid exterior walls entirely wherever possible, routing supply lines through interior walls and conditioned spaces. That design choice adds cost and isn't standard practice in a climate where hard freezes are the exception rather than the rule, which is part of why Georgia homes end up with more exposed pipe by default.

The insurance data reflects this pattern clearly: claims cluster heavily around the first hard freeze of a given winter, not evenly across the whole cold season, because that first event is the one homeowners are least prepared for and least likely to have already insulated against.

Attics are an often-overlooked risk area specific to this pattern. A supply line run through unconditioned attic space, common where plumbing serves a second-floor bathroom, sits above the home's insulation layer rather than below it, meaning it can be exposed to nearly outdoor temperatures during a hard freeze even though it's technically inside the building envelope.

A pipe that freezes but doesn't burst can still cause a real problem: ice inside the line blocks water flow entirely, which some homeowners mistake for a different plumbing failure (a closed valve, a pump issue) rather than recognizing it as a freeze. If water stops flowing to a specific fixture during a cold snap with no other explanation, a frozen (not yet burst) section of pipe is the most likely cause, and it's worth ruling that out before assuming a more complicated problem.

Outdoor spigots and hose bibs deserve specific mention, since they're often the very first thing to freeze given their direct exposure. A frost-free spigot, standard on many newer homes, is designed to shut water off well back inside the wall, reducing but not eliminating freeze risk if a hose is left attached, which traps water in the section that's meant to drain.

Common Questions

Should I leave my heat on if I'm traveling during a Georgia winter?

Yes. Turning the heat off entirely, even for a short trip, removes the one safeguard protecting interior plumbing. Keeping the thermostat at 55 degrees F or higher while away is a low-cost way to avoid a much larger repair.

Are older homes more at risk than newer ones?

Generally yes, since older homes are less likely to have had pipe insulation added retroactively, but a newer home with an uninsulated garage or crawlspace line is still genuinely at risk in a hard enough freeze.

What should I do if water stops flowing to one fixture during a freeze?

Leave the fixture's faucet open in the on position and apply gentle warmth if the frozen section is accessible (a hair dryer on low, never an open flame), and call if it doesn't resolve within a few hours or if you can't safely access the affected section yourself.

What This Means For You

The fix is not a renovation. Foam pipe sleeves on exposed lines, keeping cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls during a forecast freeze, and letting a faucet drip slowly on the coldest nights are all inexpensive habits that close most of this gap. None of these habits require a plumber's visit to implement, just a little advance planning once a hard freeze shows up in the forecast, which in this region usually means at least a day or two of warning from local weather coverage.

A pipe that has already frozen and split needs a same-day response, not a wait-and-see approach. If you are not sure whether a leak after a freeze is from a visible burst or something less obvious, leak detection can confirm it before more damage happens. That kind of on-site judgment call is exactly why we assess in person before quoting a fix.
