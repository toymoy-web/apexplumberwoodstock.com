Plumbing In A 1990s Towne Lake Home, Woodstock GA

Written by the Woodstock Apex Plumbing team · March 12, 2026.

Towne Lake Hills, Eagle Watch, and the surrounding sections were built mostly in the early-to-mid 1990s. That makes homes here some of the oldest in our entire service area, not the newest, even though the neighborhood itself does not look dated or feel like it's approaching 30 years of age. Well-kept landscaping and updated interiors can make a home's actual plumbing age easy to forget until something behind the walls reminds you.

A home built in 1993 to 1995 is now 30-plus years old. That is well past the typical 8-to-12-year lifespan of a water heater, meaning most of these homes are on their second, third, or even fourth unit. Any water heater still original to the house is running on borrowed time.

The supply lines themselves are usually copper or early-generation PEX, both reasonable materials, but original fittings, shutoff valves. Solder joints from a 1990s installation are also reaching an age where they can start to fail, seize, or develop small leaks that were not a concern a decade ago.

Mature landscaping is the other factor. Trees planted when these subdivisions were new are now 30 years into root growth. Root intrusion into sewer lines is a real, gradually worsening risk in exactly this kind of established neighborhood.

None of this means a 1990s Towne Lake home has hidden problems waiting to surface. It means the math on whether a proactive check is worth it looks different here than it does in a home built in 2015.

A practical way to check your own water heater's real age without guessing: look for the manufacture date stamped on a sticker on the tank, usually near the top, often encoded in the serial number if not written plainly. That date, not how long you've personally owned the house, is what tells you where the unit actually sits in its service life.

For plumbing work on a home this age, the code edition that applies to a permitted repair or replacement is the one the jurisdiction has adopted today, so ask the permitting office what applies. That matters specifically for a full repipe or water heater replacement, where the permitting office can tell you what the new work has to meet.

A practical maintenance schedule for a 30-year-old home like this: an annual water heater flush, a sewer camera inspection every 3 to 5 years given the mature tree cover. Prompt attention to any drop in water pressure, which is often the first sign of an aging supply line narrowing from the inside. None of these is expensive on its own. The value comes from doing them consistently rather than only after something has already gone wrong.

Fixture-level plumbing (faucets, toilets, shower valves) from the original 1990s build is also worth a look even if it's never given any trouble. A shutoff valve under a sink that hasn't been turned in 30 years can seize when you finally need it during an emergency, precisely the moment you can least afford it not to work. Exercising these valves periodically, turning them closed and back open, keeps them functional.

Homeowners association records, where the neighborhood has one, sometimes include notes on any community-wide plumbing issues from decades past, a batch of a specific fitting that failed prematurely, for example. That record is worth checking specifically to see whether it points to a fix or replacement you should plan for on your own line.

A practical budget line worth setting aside for any 30-year-old home in this neighborhood: roughly $1,500 to $2,500 held in reserve for a water heater replacement that could come at any time, plus a smaller amount for a sewer camera inspection every few years.

Treating these as predictable, budgeted maintenance rather than surprise expenses changes how disruptive they feel when they do come up, the same way a car owner budgets for tires and brakes rather than being surprised by them.

What To Do If An Older Home's Pipe Starts To Leak

  • Find your main water shutoff now, before you need it, and make sure it turns.
  • If a pipe starts leaking, close the main valve and open a low faucet to relieve pressure.
  • Move belongings away from the wet area and take photos of the damage.
  • Do not use electrical outlets or appliances near standing water.
  • Call us so we can find the source and tell you whether it is a spot repair or a sign of wider wear.

Permit Questions For Older Towne Lake Homes

Homes in Towne Lake that date from the 1990s are now old enough that original water heaters and fixtures are reaching the end of their service lives. When you replace one, ask the permitting office for your address whether a permit is needed. Woodstock's Building & Safety Division is the starting point for homes inside the city.

Common Questions

How do I find my water heater's actual installation date?

Check the manufacturer's data plate on the tank, usually a sticker near the top with a serial number that encodes the manufacture date, or often the date written in plain text alongside the model number.

Is a 30-year-old home in Towne Lake a red flag when buying?

Not by itself, but it's a reason to ask specifically about water heater age and request a sewer camera inspection rather than assuming an established, well-kept neighborhood means the plumbing is equally current.

Should I replace all my original fixtures at once, or wait for them to fail?

There's no need to replace working fixtures preemptively. The exceptions are the water heater, given its predictable service life. Any shutoff valve that feels stiff or won't fully close, both of which are worth addressing proactively rather than waiting for a failure.

Does a water heater replacement in Towne Lake need a permit?

It can, depending on where the home is located and what the job involves. Ask the permitting office for your address, and we can tell you what we have seen required.

What This Means For You

If your Towne Lake home still has its original water heater, or you cannot remember the last time it was replaced, that is worth a look before it fails on its own schedule rather than yours. The same goes for a sewer line that has never had a camera inspection in 30 years of mature tree growth overhead. A proactive look at both costs far less than an emergency response to either.

A proactive water heater replacement before a failure is almost always less disruptive than an emergency one. The same logic applies to a sewer line camera inspection in an older, tree-lined neighborhood like this one. That kind of on-site judgment call is exactly why we assess in person before quoting a fix.
