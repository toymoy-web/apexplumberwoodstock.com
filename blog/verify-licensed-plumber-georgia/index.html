How To Verify A Plumber Is Actually Licensed In Georgia

By the Woodstock Apex Plumbing team · February 12, 2026

Georgia plumbing licensing works differently than some states, which makes it worth understanding rather than just taking a company's word for it. The framework is statewide, not city-by-city, and that is actually useful information when you are checking someone out, regardless of which part of the state you happen to live in.

Plumbing licensure in Georgia falls under the State Construction Industry Licensing Board, a statewide framework rather than a patchwork of city or county-level licenses. That is different from some states; California's CSLB and Pennsylvania's municipal-only system both work differently, so a Georgia license genuinely means something consistent no matter which city you are in.

A real license is checkable, not just a claim on a website or a truck decal. The state licensing board maintains a public license lookup where you can verify a specific license number belongs to a real, current, active license before work begins, not after.

A business operating without checking this, or one that is cagey about giving you a license number to look up, is a real red flag, separate from how professional they seem on the phone. A legitimate licensed contractor has no reason to avoid the question.

Licensing is not the only thing worth checking, insurance matters too, but it is the one most homeowners skip because it takes an extra step. That extra step is a two-minute lookup, not a demanding request.

Georgia's licensing framework distinguishes between a few different license classes depending on the scope of work, and a company advertising broad plumbing services should hold the class of license that actually covers what they're doing on your home, not just any active license. Asking which specific license class applies to your job is a reasonable follow-up question.

Insurance is the other half of the equation licensing alone doesn't cover. A licensed plumber can still lack adequate liability or workers' compensation insurance, which matters if something goes wrong during the job, a flooded basement from an accidental cut line, or an injury on your property. Asking to see proof of current insurance, not just a claim of having it, is a reasonable request before major work begins.

Online reviews are useful context but shouldn't replace the license check entirely; a business can accumulate positive reviews over years while letting a license lapse, or reviews can be manipulated in ways a state license lookup simply can't be. Treat reviews as one input among several, not the deciding factor on their own.

A specialty license consideration worth knowing: gas line work and backflow prevention testing sometimes require additional certifications beyond the base plumbing license itself. If your job involves either, it's reasonable to ask specifically whether the technician holds that additional credential, not just the general plumbing license.

It's worth keeping a record of the license number you verified along with the date you checked it, particularly for a larger job spanning multiple visits. Licenses can occasionally lapse mid-project for reasons unrelated to the specific contractor's competence, an insurance lapse, an administrative renewal delay, and having your own verification date on file is useful if any question comes up later. That small habit costs nothing and can matter a great deal if a dispute ever arises over who did what work and under what authority.

What To Do Before You Call

Never pay the full amount upfront

A legitimate licensed contractor typically asks for a reasonable deposit, not full payment before any work begins. Being pressured to pay in full upfront, especially in cash, is a common warning sign of an unlicensed or fraudulent operator.

Get a written estimate before work starts

A verbal-only quote with nothing in writing leaves you with no recourse if the final bill differs significantly from what was discussed. A written estimate protects both parties.

Be wary of high-pressure, same-day-only pricing

Legitimate emergency dispatch does mean fast response, but a company using artificial urgency ("this price is only good if you sign right now") to prevent you from checking their license or getting a second opinion is a red flag separate from genuine emergency service.

Common Questions

What happens if I hire an unlicensed plumber?

Beyond the quality-of-work risk, unlicensed work can create real problems at resale (a home inspector or buyer's attorney may flag unpermitted plumbing work) and may not be covered by homeowners insurance if something goes wrong as a result.

Does a license number expire?

Yes, Georgia plumbing licenses require periodic renewal. The state board's lookup tool shows current status, not just whether a number was ever issued, which is exactly why checking it directly matters more than trusting an old business card or truck decal.

Is a handyman allowed to do plumbing work in Georgia?

Only within narrow limits; most substantive plumbing work legally requires a licensed plumber, not a general handyman, regardless of how minor the job seems. When in doubt, ask directly which license covers the specific work being done.

What This Means For You

Before hiring any plumber for a non-emergency job, ask for their license number and take the two minutes to verify it through the state board's lookup tool. For an emergency, it is reasonable to verify after the fact, but still worth doing. A legitimate company will never treat that request as an inconvenience.

Licensing matters most on the jobs with real code implications, like a full water heater installation requiring a permit, or a sewer line repair that has to meet county inspection standards. That kind of on-site judgment call is exactly why we assess in person before quoting a fix.
