Tank Or Tankless? What Actually Makes Sense For A Woodstock-Area Home

By the Woodstock Apex Plumbing team · March 5, 2026

This is a genuine decision, not just an upsell, and the right answer depends more on your household's actual hot water demand than on which option sounds more modern, so it's worth working through the real numbers, upfront cost, expected lifespan, and maintenance, before committing either way rather than choosing based on which option a salesperson happens to prefer that day.

A tank water heater costs less upfront ($1,400 to $2,600 installed for a standard 40-50 gallon unit) and is generally simple to service. Its real limitation is capacity: once the tank runs out, you wait for it to reheat, which matters in a household running multiple showers, a dishwasher, and laundry close together.

A tankless unit costs more upfront ($2,800 to $4,200 installed) but heats water on demand rather than storing a fixed amount, meaning it genuinely does not run out, provided it is sized correctly for the home's simultaneous demand.

Sizing is where tankless most often goes wrong. An undersized unit does not just run slower, it genuinely cannot keep up with two showers and a dishwasher running at once, and that failure shows up as a real capacity problem, not an occasional inconvenience. Sizing to the home's real fixture count and typical simultaneous use matters more than a generic square-footage estimate.

The math tends to favor tankless most clearly in larger households with high simultaneous hot water demand, common in the bigger homes going up around Towne Lake and Holly Springs, and favors a standard tank in a smaller household or a smaller downtown home where the tank rarely runs dry anyway. Neither choice is objectively better in the abstract; the right one depends entirely on how your specific household actually uses hot water day to day.

Operating cost is a real, if secondary, factor worth including in the decision. A tankless unit only heats water when it's actually being drawn, avoiding the standby heat loss a tank experiences continuously keeping stored water hot, which can translate into a modest but real energy savings over the unit's lifespan.

Retrofit cost is another practical consideration many homeowners underestimate: converting an existing gas tank installation to gas tankless sometimes requires a larger gas line and a dedicated vent the original tank never needed, adding to the upfront installation cost beyond just the unit price difference between the two options.

Maintenance expectations differ too, and it's worth factoring in honestly. A tank needs an occasional flush but is otherwise low-maintenance for its whole service life. A tankless unit needs periodic descaling, roughly every 12 to 18 months in this region's water, to perform at its rated capacity; skipping that maintenance is the single most common reason a tankless owner ends up disappointed with a system that was correctly sized in the first place.

Space savings are a real, if secondary, factor for some homeowners: a tankless unit mounts on a wall and takes up a fraction of the floor space a standard 40-to-50-gallon tank requires, which can matter in a smaller mechanical closet or a home converting a former water heater closet into usable storage.

It's worth having a real conversation about future plans too. A household that expects to grow, an addition, a finished basement bathroom, a family expansion, may want to size any new water heater, tank or tankless, with that future demand in mind rather than replacing again in a few years once the household's real needs change.

Neither option is inherently the "right" answer for every home, and being skeptical of a contractor who pushes one option hard regardless of your household's actual situation is reasonable. A genuinely honest recommendation should follow from your specific fixture count, household size, and hot water habits, not a standard upsell script applied to every customer.

Common Questions

How long does a tankless water heater last compared to a tank?

A well-maintained tankless unit often lasts 15 to 20 years, compared to 8 to 12 for a standard tank, though that assumes regular descaling; a neglected tankless unit in hard water can fail well short of that range.

Can I install a tankless water heater in an older downtown Woodstock home?

Usually yes, but expect a real conversation about gas line sizing and venting requirements first, since older homes weren't built with tankless installation in mind the way newer construction increasingly is.

Do I need a bigger tankless unit if I want two showers running at once?

Yes, sizing has to account for simultaneous demand, not just peak single-fixture flow. A unit sized only for one shower will genuinely struggle, or fail to keep up, when a second fixture draws hot water at the same time.

What This Means For You

If you are replacing a failed unit under time pressure, a like-for-like tank swap is usually the fastest same-day option. If you have time to plan ahead of a failure, it is worth a real conversation about your household's actual hot water use before deciding. Either path is reasonable; the goal is simply making the choice deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever the last homeowner happened to have installed.
