Georgia Ranks #1 in the Nation for Frozen Pipe Insurance Claims

By the Woodstock Apex Plumbing team · January 8, 2026

It sounds backwards: a state known for mild winters leading the country in frozen pipe damage. But according to State Farm, Georgia homeowners filed more frozen-pipe insurance claims than any other state between August 2022 and August 2023, about $100 million in claims, roughly 12.4% of the insurer's entire national total for that period.

The reason isn't that Georgia gets colder than states with real winters. It's the opposite: homes here are built for heat, not cold, and the state's cold snaps arrive suddenly rather than gradually, giving homeowners far less warning than a place where freezing temperatures are the seasonal norm.

Pipes in northern states are typically buried deeper and run through walls with cold-climate insulation standards built in from the start. A lot of Georgia plumbing, especially in crawlspaces, garages, and exterior walls, was never insulated with a hard freeze in mind, because for most winters, it never needed to be. When a real cold snap hits, that gap shows up fast.

The Georgia Insurance Commissioner's office cites a separate figure worth knowing: the average insurance claim for frozen pipe damage runs around $18,000, once you count the water damage itself, not just the pipe repair. That's a genuinely large number for a problem that a few dollars of foam pipe insulation can often prevent.

Standard homeowners insurance in Georgia generally covers sudden, accidental water damage from a burst pipe, but coverage can be affected by whether reasonable prevention steps were taken (keeping the heat on, insulating exposed lines). Check your specific policy rather than assuming either way.

Insurance adjusters looking at a frozen-pipe claim generally want to see that reasonable prevention steps were taken, not perfection. Keeping the home heated to a minimum safe temperature and having exposed pipes insulated are the two most commonly cited factors, and neither requires a major investment to satisfy.

Georgia's exposure to this risk is compounded by how many homes here have plumbing running through unconditioned spaces, garages, crawlspaces, and attics, that would be insulated as standard practice in a colder-climate state. That's not a defect specific to any one builder; it reflects decades of construction decisions made for a climate that rarely demanded otherwise.

The $100 million figure from State Farm covers just one insurer over a single 12-month period, which means the true statewide total across all carriers is considerably larger. That scale is worth keeping in mind against the relatively small cost of prevention: a few dollars of foam pipe insulation per exposed section, applied once, protects against a claim that averages $18,000 when it does happen.

The $18,000 average claim figure from the Georgia Insurance Commissioner's office also likely understates the full cost for many homeowners, since it reflects the insured, reimbursed portion. Deductibles, items not covered under a given policy, and the disruption of living around repair work for days or weeks aren't captured in that number, which is another reason prevention is worth more than the raw dollar figure suggests. Comparing that number to the cost of a single box of foam pipe sleeves puts the math in real perspective, and it's a comparison worth making before the next hard freeze, not after.

A homeowner renovating or adding onto an older Woodstock home has a real opportunity to close this gap permanently: any time a wall or ceiling is already open for other work, adding pipe insulation to an exposed run costs very little in materials and labor compared to doing it as a standalone project later.

Homeowners who grew up in or previously lived in a colder-winter state sometimes bring useful, already-formed habits with them to Georgia, dripping faucets before a freeze, keeping cabinet doors open, without needing to be taught. If that's not your background, it's worth treating this article's steps as a genuine checklist to follow the first time a hard freeze is forecast, rather than trusting instinct alone.

Common Questions

How cold does it actually need to get for pipes to freeze in Georgia?

Exposed, uninsulated pipe can begin freezing once temperatures drop into the mid-20s Fahrenheit for several consecutive hours, especially with wind exposure. A single overnight cold snap is enough; it doesn't take a sustained deep freeze.

Does letting a faucet drip actually help?

Yes. Moving water is significantly more resistant to freezing than still water sitting in a pipe, and a slow drip relieves pressure buildup even if some freezing does occur, reducing the chance of a full burst.

Is foam pipe insulation something I can install myself?

Yes, for accessible pipe in a garage, crawlspace, or basement, foam pipe sleeves are inexpensive and simple to fit yourself. Pipe run inside finished walls or hard-to-reach attic space is better left to a professional during a broader inspection.

What This Means For You

If a hard freeze is forecast for Woodstock, the cheap fixes are the same ones northern homeowners take for granted: keep the thermostat at 55 degrees F or higher even when away, let a faucet drip on exposed lines, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, and know where your main shutoff valve is before you need it in a hurry.

A pipe that has already burst needs immediate attention rather than a wait-and-see approach; call our 24/7 line the moment you find one. If the freeze also knocked out your water heater's pilot or thermostat, that is a straightforward water heater repair rather than a full replacement in most cases. That kind of on-site judgment call is exactly why we assess in person before quoting a fix.

This is a genuinely sitewide risk, not a downtown-only issue: even the newer, well-insulated homes around Towne Lake have exposed lines in garages and crawlspaces that can freeze in a hard enough snap. Reach out and we can confirm your exact service area on the call.
