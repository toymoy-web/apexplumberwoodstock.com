Repiping & Whole-Home Pipe Replacement in Woodstock, GA

Repiping means replacing a home's supply lines throughout, typically with PEX or copper, rather than continuing to patch an aging system one failure at a time. It's a real, significant decision, and we only recommend it when the underlying pipe material itself is the actual problem.

Tired Of Repairing The Same Old Pipes Over And Over?

If you've had more than one or two isolated pipe leaks in the same older home over a short span, or persistently low water pressure throughout the house rather than at just one fixture, that pattern often points to a system-wide problem with the pipe material itself, not a string of unrelated bad luck. The branded fix for that is a repipe.

Galvanized steel pipe, common in Woodstock homes built before the 2000s building boom, corrodes from the inside out, gradually narrowing the pipe's effective diameter. That shows up as declining water pressure over years and an increasing frequency of pinhole leaks as the corrosion progresses toward failure.

A single isolated leak in an otherwise sound system is a repair, not a reason to repipe. The signal for a full repipe is a pattern: multiple leaks in different spots over a relatively short time, persistent low pressure throughout the house, or visibly rust-colored water even after running a tap for a minute.

PEX and copper are the two standard modern materials. PEX costs less and is more forgiving of a freeze (it can expand slightly without bursting in some cases), while copper has a longer track record and is preferred in some situations, exposed runs, for example. We'll recommend based on your home's specific layout, not a default answer.

A whole-home repipe is disruptive but usually completed faster than most homeowners expect, often within a few days for a typical single-family home, with wall access points patched afterward (drywall/paint touch-up is typically a separate line item from the plumbing work itself).

Partial repiping, replacing only the sections showing the worst corrosion rather than the entire system, is sometimes a reasonable middle step, though it comes with the honest caveat that the remaining original pipe will likely need attention eventually too.

A repipe performed under permit meets current Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements regardless of what code applied when the original galvanized system was installed decades ago, which matters for both safety and for a future sale of the home.

During a repipe, water service is typically interrupted for at least part of a day while the old lines are removed and new ones installed; we coordinate timing so a household can plan around at least one bathroom or a temporary water source rather than being caught without any water access at all.

A repipe decision usually follows a pattern first identified during leak detection or repeated general repair visits on the same aging system. Once a repipe is complete, a water heater replacement is often a reasonable next step if the unit is also original to the home.

What To Check Before We Arrive

Know where your temporary water access will be

Confirm with your technician which fixture, if any, stays functional during the repipe, so you can plan the day around it rather than being caught without any water access.

Keep pets and children away from open wall sections

A repipe temporarily opens wall or ceiling sections to access old lines. Keep those areas off-limits until the work is complete and patched.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I need a repipe versus a single repair?

A single isolated leak is usually a repair. A pattern of multiple leaks in different spots, persistent low pressure throughout the house, or rust-colored water are the real signals a full repipe is worth considering.

PEX or copper: which is better?

Both are reasonable modern materials. PEX costs less and handles some freeze events better; copper has a longer track record and suits certain exposed-run situations. We recommend based on your home's specific layout, not a default answer.

How long does a whole-home repipe take?

Often just a few days for a typical single-family home, with drywall and paint touch-up at access points usually handled as a separate step afterward.
